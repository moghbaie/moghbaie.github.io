Which AI labs depend most on a single chip-supply firm?

Nearly all US AI labs' compute runs through one company, TSMC, even for labs that buy from four different chip makers.

AI labs compete on compute, and every chip they run passes through a short chain of firms: a chip designer, a chip factory, a memory maker and a packager. If one of those firms stopped, how much of each lab's compute would go with it? Haddy Alchaer mapped how fragile the chip industry as a whole is; this study asks the same question lab by lab, with a model in which a chip needs a factory and memory and packaging. The answer: for US-aligned labs, buying from several chip makers barely matters, because their chips meet again at TSMC; of the three supply steps behind every chip, memory is the only one spread across several firms; and labs differ mostly in what they depend on next.

Buying from more chip makers doesn't spread the risk

Meta and Anthropic each buy from four chip families, yet in this model they would lose 100% of their compute if TSMC stopped, as much as xAI (SpaceX), which buys from one. Across all 8 US-aligned labs the loss would be 96%–100%: whichever chip a lab buys, the chip is made in TSMC's factories and, except for Cerebras' wafer-scale chips, packaged with TSMC's CoWoS.

Memory is the one step that's spread out

Of the three supply steps behind every chip, only memory is spread across several firms: each lab's memory behaves like 2.0 or more equal suppliers, against at most 1.1 for chip factories and 1.6 for packaging. If the largest, SK hynix, stopped, these labs would lose 41%–65% of their compute in this model: a heavy blow, but not a total one, because Samsung and Micron supply the rest. Labs differ mainly in their second-biggest risk

Set TSMC aside and the labs separate. Their next-biggest single dependency: OpenAI, Meta, xAI (SpaceX), Microsoft AI and Mistral on Nvidia GPU; Anthropic on SK hynix; Google DeepMind on Google TPU; Amazon AGI on AWS Trainium. OpenAI's Cerebras chips (6% of its compute) are the only route around TSMC's packaging. OpenAI would also keep its custom chips' 14% only if their unidentified packager is not TSMC, so that part is an assumption, not a finding.

Method

1 · The network

We model the supply chain as a network. Each node is a lab, a chip family or a firm. Each link means "buys from" and carries two labels: what is bought (chips, chip factory, memory or packaging) and the share, the supplier's fraction of this buyer's purchases of that item. For example, share(Nvidia → SK hynix, memory) = 62% means an estimated 62% of Nvidia's HBM memory comes from SK hynix.

The layers follow Haddy Alchaer's seven-layer model with AI labs added on top: labs → chips → packaging → chip factories and memory → equipment, wafers, gases, raw materials. Only the first four are used to compare labs: every Western factory buys upstream inputs from the same firms, so those layers cannot tell labs apart.

2 · Recipes: OR inside a step, AND across steps

A chip needs a factory and memory and packaging. Suppliers within a step can replace each other (OR); different steps cannot (AND). Try it in Figure 5: switch suppliers off and watch the loss reach the labs. If each supplier f keeps a fraction af of its output:

step availability = sum of share x a; chip output = min over steps
Example (from the data)SK hynix stops (a = 0); Samsung and Micron keep going. Memory available to Nvidia = 0.62·0 + 0.27·1 + 0.11·1 = 0.38. Factory = 1, packaging = 1, so Nvidia's output = min(1, 0.38, 1) = 0.38.

Alchaer's max-flow model adds flows together, so it cannot express this AND; that is why it finds no loss of flow when TSMC is removed.

3 · Exposure: how much of a lab depends on a firm

Exposure follows every path from a lab to a firm and multiplies the shares along it. It divides only by the compute that actually needs that step (Cerebras chips use no HBM, so they are not counted when measuring memory).

exposure = sum of share(lab to chip) x share(chip to firm) / compute needing the step
Example (from the data)Anthropic's chips: Google TPU 41%, Nvidia GPU 27%, AWS Trainium 21%, AMD Instinct 11%, all made by TSMC. Exposure(Anthropic, TSMC) = 0.41·1 + 0.27·1 + 0.21·1 + 0.11·1 = 100%.

Amkor and ASE's CoWoS work cannot run without TSMC's CoWoS step, so dependence on them is also counted as dependence on CoWoS (indirect dependence).

4 · Concentration: the effective number of suppliers

For each lab and step, the Herfindahl–Hirschman index (the sum of squared exposures) is turned into an effective number of suppliers. Squaring makes large suppliers dominate.

HHI = sum of exposure squared; N_eff = 1 / HHI
Illustration (not study data)Suppliers at 60%, 25% and 15%: HHI = 0.36 + 0.0625 + 0.0225 = 0.445, so Neff = 2.2, "behaves like 2.2 equal suppliers". An even three-way split gives 3; one supplier gives 1. In the data, Anthropic's memory behaves like 2.5 suppliers. An unidentified supplier counts as one supplier (the cautious choice), and its share is always shown.

5 · Critical-firm loss: what one failure costs

loss = 1 - sum of share x chip output without the firm
Example (from the data)If TSMC CoWoS stops, Anthropic loses 99% of its compute in this model. A lab's critical firm is the one with the largest loss. "No rerouting within a year" is assumed: leading-edge factories, HBM and CoWoS are sold out through 2027.

6 · The shared-firm rule (fixed in advance)

A firm is a shared dependency for a group of labs if every lab in the group depends on it for at least 95% of a step. Shared firms are reported once and left out when ranking labs, because a firm every lab depends on cannot tell them apart. The rule and its threshold are in the pre-registered plan.

7 · Uncertainty

Every share has a likely range set by a fixed rule: a company statement ±5 points; conflicting reports span each other; deals and dated capacity give a firm low and an uncertain high; no source gives ±50%. Every share is drawn at random within its range 1,000 times (each buyer's shares re-scaled to 100%) and everything is recomputed. Unidentified suppliers are treated two ways: they keep working (lower bound), or they are assumed to be the firm that stops (upper bound). A ranking claim is made only if it holds in at least 90% of draws under both. Otherwise labs are tied. Details and the four dated amendments: pre-registered plan.

How sure we are

Most numbers here are estimates. Only xAI states its chip mix outright; the other mixes are pieced together from deals and reports, and memory splits come from industry reporting, not from the companies. So we re-ran everything with 1,000 alternative sets of estimates, each share drawn from its likely range.

Findings 1 and 2 hold in every one of them. A TSMC stop never costs any US-aligned lab less than 81% of its compute (Mistral is the low one, because some of its chips are unidentified; every other lab stays at 93% or more). An SK hynix stop always costs between 24% and 76%, never close to all of it. Finding 3 is less certain: Google DeepMind, xAI (SpaceX), Microsoft AI and Mistral keep the same second-biggest risk in every draw, but 8 of the 28 lab-to-lab comparisons are too close to call.

Before collecting data we registered two predictions about how labs would rank; neither held. The full record, with every later change dated, is in the pre-registered plan.

Chinese labs

We found no Chinese lab's chip mix in a quick search, so every share for these labs is a rough estimate with a wide range, and they are kept out of the comparisons above. They run on a mix of Huawei Ascend chips made by SMIC, their own designs and licensed Nvidia chips, so their dependence is spread differently from US labs': on SMIC and Huawei, and on export licences that could be withdrawn.

What this doesn't measure

We don't measure dependence on cloud and data-center operators (Microsoft, AWS, Google, Oracle, CoreWeave). That's a separate risk: contracts, power and sites. A lab spread across several chips can still depend heavily on one operator.

Dependence is not likelihood: relying on one stable firm may be safer than spreading across fragile ones. "Compute lost" assumes no rerouting within a year, so it is an upper bound over longer horizons. Lab chip mixes are reconstructed from deals, not disclosed.
